# Stage: write

Write one file per epic and per story in the approved map. The inventory is the index, not the whole source: for each item a story covers, open the prototype file and lines the inventory cites and read the logic behind it. The rule a builder needs (how two names are matched, what clearing a field does) is usually in the code and rarely in a one-line inventory statement.

Before a story is called written, run [builders-checklist.md](builders-checklist.md) over it. Every line that applies is either answered in the story or cited as an open question.

**When neither the inventory nor the code answers something a story needs**, that is a question, not a licence to invent. A story with any open question is `needs-info`. Writers read the prototype but do not edit the inventory or the open-questions file, so several can work in parallel: a rule a writer finds in the code goes into the story (Trace ID `NEW` until the closing pass numbers it) and into its report as `inventory addition: <statement> — <file:line>`, and a question goes into the story as `NEW-Q-A: <question>`, lettered so a requirement can point at it. The closing pass adds the items, numbers the questions and puts the IDs back into the stories.

## The reader

Two readers, neither of whom has seen the prototype's code or this conversation:

- a developer or coding agent building on a different stack, who needs the behavior and its rules;
- a tester or client, who needs to know what to do and what they should see.

Each story must stand alone for both, read together with three short companions it links in its header: the epic, the standing requirements and the feature's terms. The test: handed those and nothing else, could a developer start, know exactly what is still open, and could a tester sign it off?

**What a story settles and what it leaves.** A story states behavior a person can observe and the rules behind it. It does not design the build: data schemas, service interfaces, synchronous or not, component choice and storage are the team's to decide, and a story that prescribes them is describing the prototype. So a requirement says "the import records who added it, the day, the document's name and each change" and not which columns hold them. The questions a story must not leave unacknowledged are the ones about behavior; a question about how to build it is not a defect.

## Terms and fixtures, written first

Before any story, write `<out>/fixtures.md` (see UAT script below) and `<out>/terms.md`: every word the stories will use that a newcomer would not know. That is the feature's own vocabulary (the names of its steps, states and objects, such as "check step" or "Already on file"), the glossary terms it leans on, and a legend for the notation (`R`, `S`, the inventory ID prefixes, `Q-n`, `spec #n`, and where each can be looked up) and for the tags. Say plainly that every numbered requirement is binding whatever its tag: the tag records where it came from (`shown` means observed in the prototype and not yet confirmed in writing), not how optional it is. One or two sentences each. Stories use these words exactly and never define them again. A product-wide noun the glossary lacks is defined here for now and listed as a glossary proposal; once the glossary has it, the glossary wins and the entry here becomes a pointer.

## Story

Use [assets/story.md](../assets/story.md). Section by section:

- **Title**: actor and outcome, in the glossary's terms, in about 70 characters; shorten the map's line, which may be longer. Not a screen name. The file's slug is three to five words from the title.
- **Story line**: As a / I want / so that. The "so that" is the actor's reason, taken from the goal in the map.
- **Description**: two to five sentences. Where this sits in the workflow, what comes before and after, and where to see it: the prototype route with a sample record filled in, or "not in the prototype" for behavior that was decided and never built.
- **Readiness**: one line under the header block saying what can start now and what cannot, and why: `Can start: R1, R2, R5. Waiting: R3, R4 on the gis-data-sources feature (not built); R6 on Q-56.` It must agree with `Blocked by`: a requirement that needs an enabler can start only against a stand-in, and the line says so. A reader of a `needs-info` story must not have to work this out.
- **Requirements**: numbered `R1…`. One observable behavior or rule each, stated as what the system does: `R3. A proposed value the reading is unsure of is left empty and flagged.` Keep the line clean for a human reader; its tag and inventory IDs go in the **Trace** table in the collapsed notes (`R3 | decided | RUL-7`), which is what sync and the coverage check read. A requirement resting on items with different tags lists them in matching order, separated by semicolons (`decided; shown | RUL-20; RUL-72`). Something the prototype does by accident (no guard against a second press, a duplicate saved) is not a requirement: write "Not decided: … (Q-n)" on the requirement it touches. Tags are the inventory's: `shown`, `decided`, `decided, not built`, and `inferred` only with an open question beside it. When part of a requirement waits on an open question, state the known part, mark it `(open: Q-n)`, and write the scenario for the known part. A requirement that leans on an item another story covers cites that story (`relies on story 01, STA-26`) and leaves the item out of this story's `Covers`. Include the states that apply (empty, error, no permission). **Cross the states.** A story that gives an element a new state must say what happens where that state meets the others the same element can be in (a kept change whose value differs, a kept change the reading also flagged, every change kept so nothing is left to add), and which wins. A prototype shows each state alone, so these meetings are where a builder gets stuck: list the element's states from the inventory, and for each pair either state the outcome or cite the question that asks it. Describe behavior, not the prototype's layout or component names; the build stack is different. Interface wording quoted from the prototype is the required wording unless the requirement says otherwise; write a placeholder as `<source name>`, never in italics. When a requirement depends on another feature, say what this story needs from it (what it asks, what it gets back, what it hands over) and whether that feature exists; if it does not, the requirement is listed as waiting.
- **Out of scope**: what a reader might assume is included, and which story has it. A control or case another story owns is named with that story's number and nothing more: the standing requirements carry the one rule for all of them (it is absent, or the case is not handled, until its story ships). Do not invent in-between behavior per story; nobody decided it, and it would be a requirement that exists only because of how the work was cut.
- **What this story needs to know from elsewhere**: a rule owned by another story or feature that decides how this one behaves is stated here in one line with its owner ("Added entries are saved unpublished; publishing belongs to the publish-review feature"). An enabler this story calls is cited with what information goes in and what comes back, in a sentence or two and with no mechanics. Pointing at the owner without stating the rule is not enough: the reader has only this file.
- **Scenarios**: Given/When/Then, one behavior each; a Then that checks several unrelated things is several scenarios. If one-behavior scenarios take the story well past 12, look for a split that leaves both halves demonstrable alone (see slice, "Splitting after writing"); if there is none, the story stays whole. Each cites the requirement it proves (`proves R3`). Every requirement has at least one scenario. A wording variant (the singular of a count) may sit in its requirement without a scenario of its own when no fixture produces it, or produces it only through a story outside `Blocked by`. A requirement about something recorded but not yet shown anywhere belongs to the story that records it, with a scenario, and its UAT step says which later story makes it visible. A scenario may use only behavior from this story and the stories in its `Blocked by`, including what those are blocked by in turn; one that needs a later story to set up its Given belongs in that later story. Use the inventory's Sample data so a scenario can be run against the prototype, and name the data in **Test data**; where the inventory records none, describe the data by kind and size and never invent realistic-looking names. More than about 12 scenarios is the signal to look for a split: say so in your report instead of trimming.
- **UAT script**: the same behavior for a person at a keyboard, who has never seen the prototype's code. Say where to start, which sample to use, and what to note before acting if a later step compares against it. Every step is something the tester can do and an outcome they can see; never ask them to prove an absence without naming what to try. If part of the script cannot be run yet, say so in one line at the top and stop the script where it stops being runnable. Notes about how the prototype fakes things belong in Agent notes, never in Test data. The script is written for the built product; how to walk the same paths on the prototype is said once, in `fixtures.md`. Sample records, files and their expected results live once in `<out>/fixtures.md`, written with the terms file before any story; Test data names the fixture and the rows a step compares against. Where the feature depends on something not deterministic (a model's reading), the fixture is a recorded result and the script says it is run against that recording. For `Reset`, write "none needed" when the script changes nothing, how to return to the starting state when it does, or the question's ID when nobody knows. Preconditions, test data, then numbered steps, each with one action and one expected result, in plain language with the interface's real labels. It walks the main path and the one or two exceptions a client would care about; it does not repeat every scenario. If the result can only be seen through another story (a note that only the reviewer sees), name that story under Preconditions.
- **Open questions**: only the Q-IDs whose answer would change a requirement, scenario or step in this story, one line each saying which and how. A question attached to a covered item that changes nothing here is left out. Never restate the question. Write "None." when there are none. A story with open questions has `Status: needs-info`.
- **Notes for whoever builds it**: collapsed. The Trace table first, then Prototype file pointers, data shapes as the prototype models them, and anything in the prototype that should *not* be copied (the artifacts). A note that says the built product must behave differently from the prototype is a requirement: move it up.

Header lines: `Type`, `Epic`, `Release`, `Status`, `Revision: 1`, `Blocked by`, `Covers` (inventory IDs), `Sources` (route; spec decision numbers or the interview Q-IDs the story rests on), `Snapshot` ("not yet captured" until baseline fills it).

Agent notes are written from the inventory: full file paths from its Scope list, and data shapes from its Objects table. That is all they need to be; the developer has the prototype to open.

## A story the prototype does not show

Some stories come from a decision alone: a `later` item, or a promise the prototype never kept. Such a story cannot pass the stand-alone test yet, so write it as a stub and say so: the story line, the rules that are decided, and under Open questions everything that must be decided before it can be built. Its status is `needs-info`, and it carries scenarios only for the rules that are settled. A stub keeps the header, the story line, the decided rules, Out of scope and Open questions, and drops the UAT script.

## Epic

Use [assets/epic.md](../assets/epic.md). The epic carries what no single story should repeat: the goal, the actors, the decisions that shape every story in it, what is out of scope, and the story list. List stories by dependency (an enabler before what it unblocks), which may differ from their numbers. An epic's `Status` is `needs-info` while any of its stories is; its `Release` is its stories' value, or `mixed`. Name the epic as the map does (a letter or a number).

## Enabler

Same template as a story, `Type: enabler`. Rename the Story section **Capability** and state what it provides and which stories it unblocks. Scenarios are still required; they describe what becomes possible once it exists. Rename the UAT script **Verification** and write the steps a developer or tester takes to confirm it, through the screen of a story it unblocks where there is one; name that story under Preconditions. An enabler that a recorded fixture stands in for elsewhere cannot be verified against that recording: say what real input it is verified with.

## Naming

`<out>/stories/NN-slug.md`, numbered as in the map. A story runs to roughly 100 to 220 lines; well past that, look for build design, restated fixtures or a second story hiding inside. `<out>/epics/E-<id>-slug.md`.

## After the stories are written

Writers edit only their own story files, so one closing pass brings the shared files back in line: number any new questions, fix pointers the splits left stale, rebuild the map's story table and the epics from the story headers, and run `scripts/coverage.py` (see the check stage) until map, epics and stories agree.

## Rules that span stories

The map gives each inventory item to one story. When a rule has parts that can only be shown through different stories (a rule about every kind of change, where each kind arrives in its own story), each part is stated and proven in the story that can show it, and the inventory item is split so each story covers its own part. A rule that is whole in one story and merely used by others is stated there, and the others cite it as `relies on story NN`. Two stories that each need the other for a scenario are one story, or the joint scenario belongs to whichever is built second. Standing requirements are not re-proven in every story; a story writes a scenario for one only where it is the story's point (an error message story proves the error-message pattern).

## Done when

Every story and epic in the map has a file, no section is empty, every requirement has a scenario, and every scenario cites a requirement.
